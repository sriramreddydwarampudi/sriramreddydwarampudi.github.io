    Angel's Classification of Malocclusion is a system developed by Dr. Edward Angle (the "Father of Modern Orthodontics") to categorize different types of malocclusion (misalignment of teeth) based on the mesiodistal (anteroposterior) relationship of the first permanent molars and the position of the maxillary incisors.

    Angle’s Classification (1899)¶

    Angle’s system divides malocclusion into three main classes:

    1. Class I Malocclusion (Neutroclusion)¶

    • Molar Relationship: The mesiobuccal cusp of the maxillary first molar fits into the buccal groove of the mandibular first molar (normal relationship).
    • Problem: Despite the correct molar relationship, there may be crowding, spacing, rotations, or other dental irregularities.
    • Incisor Position: The upper incisors may be proclined (flared forward) or retroclined (tilted backward).

    2. Class II Malocclusion (Distocclusion)¶

    • Molar Relationship: The mandibular first molar is positioned posteriorly (distally) relative to the maxillary first molar.
    • Subdivisions:
    • Class II Division 1:
      • Protruded (flared) upper incisors.
      • Increased overjet.
      • Often associated with a lip trap or incompetent lips.
    • Class II Division 2:
      • Upper central incisors are retroclined (tilted backward).
      • Upper lateral incisors may be proclined or normal.
      • Deep bite (excessive overbite).
    • Skeletal Cause: Often due to a retrognathic (recessed) mandible or prognathic (protrusive) maxilla.

    3. Class III Malocclusion (Mesiocclusion)¶

    • Molar Relationship: The mandibular first molar is positioned anteriorly (mesially) relative to the maxillary first molar.
    • Incisor Relationship:
    • Anterior crossbite (lower incisors overlap upper incisors).
    • May have edge-to-edge bite or reverse overjet.
    • Skeletal Cause: Often due to a prognathic (protrusive) mandible or retrognathic (recessed) maxilla.

    Limitations of Angle’s Classification¶

    1. Does not consider vertical or transverse discrepancies (e.g., open bite, deep bite, crossbites).
    2. Ignores skeletal vs. dental causes of malocclusion.
    3. Assumes first molars are fixed reference points, which may not be true if molars are missing or drifted.

    Modern Additions¶

    • Andrews’ Six Keys of Occlusion (refined Angle’s concepts).
    • Ackerman-Proffit Classification (includes vertical and transverse dimensions).
    • Skeletal Classification (Based on ANB Angle).

    Conclusion¶

    Angle’s classification remains a foundational system in orthodontics but is often supplemented with other diagnostic tools for comprehensive treatment planning.

    Andrews’ Six Keys of Occlusion¶

    Developed by Dr. Lawrence F. Andrews in 1972, the Six Keys of Occlusion are a set of criteria for ideal static dental occlusion, based on a study of 120 non-orthodontic normal occlusions. These keys refine Angle’s classification by providing more detailed functional and aesthetic benchmarks.

    1. Molar Interarch Relationship (Key I)¶

    • The mesiobuccal cusp of the maxillary first molar occludes in the buccal groove of the mandibular first molar.
    • The distobuccal cusp of the maxillary first molar contacts the mesiobuccal cusp of the mandibular second molar.

    2. Crown Angulation (Tip) (Key II)¶

    • The gingival portion of the long axis of all crowns should be distally inclined relative to the occlusal portion.
    • This ensures proper interproximal contact and arch stability.

    3. Crown Inclination (Torque) (Key III)¶

    • Anterior Teeth: Upper incisors have positive inclination (labial root torque), while lower incisors have slightly negative or upright inclination.
    • Posterior Teeth: Buccal surfaces of upper molars and premolars are lingually inclined, while lower posteriors are buccally inclined for proper intercuspation.

    4. Absence of Rotations (Key IV)¶

    • Teeth should be free of rotations to ensure proper contact points and occlusion.

    5. Tight Contacts (Key V)¶

    • All teeth should have tight, flush contacts without spacing or crowding.

    6. Curve of Spee (Key VI)¶

    • The curve of Spee (anteroposterior curvature of the occlusal plane) should be flat or slightly curved (≤1.5 mm depth).
    • Excessive curve leads to deep bite, while reverse curve causes open bite.

    Ackerman-Proffit Classification (1969)¶

    This system expands beyond Angle’s anteroposterior (AP) classification by incorporating vertical and transverse dimensions, as well as facial profile assessment. It provides a more comprehensive 5-step diagnostic approach:

    1. Alignment (Dental Arch Form)¶

    • Evaluates crowding, spacing, rotations, and arch symmetry.

    2. Profile (AP Jaw Relationship – Angle’s Class I, II, III)¶

    • Assesses skeletal relationship (e.g., retrognathic vs. prognathic jaws).

    3. Transverse Relationship (Buccolingual)¶

    • Checks for crossbites (posterior or anterior), midlines, and arch width discrepancies.

    4. Vertical Relationship (Overbite/Open Bite)¶

    • Evaluates deep bite, open bite, or normal overbite.

    5. Dental vs. Skeletal Problem¶

    • Determines whether the malocclusion is due to tooth position (dental) or jaw discrepancy (skeletal).

    Advantages Over Angle’s Classification:¶

    • Considers all three planes of space (AP, vertical, transverse).
    • Includes facial aesthetics and soft tissue analysis.
    • Differentiates dental vs. skeletal causes.

    Clinical Usefulness of Angle’s, Andrews’, and Ackerman-Proffit Classifications in Orthodontics¶

    Orthodontic classifications help in diagnosis, treatment planning, and communication among professionals. Here’s how each system is clinically useful:

    1. Angle’s Classification of Malocclusion¶

    Clinical Usefulness:¶

    ✅ Basic Diagnosis – Quickly categorizes malocclusion into Class I, II, or III based on molar relationship.
    ✅ Treatment Planning – Guides whether the case requires:
    - Class I: Mainly alignment and bite correction (e.g., crowding, spacing).
    - Class II: Mandibular advancement (e.g., functional appliances) or maxillary retraction.
    - Class III: Maxillary protraction or mandibular setback (surgery in severe cases).
    ✅ Communication – Universal terminology for discussing cases (e.g., "Class II Div 1").

    Limitations in Clinical Use:¶

    ❌ Ignores vertical & transverse problems (e.g., deep bite, open bite, crossbites).
    ❌ Doesn’t differentiate skeletal vs. dental causes (e.g., retrognathic mandible vs. proclined incisors).

    Best for: Initial screening, simple cases, and teaching basics of occlusion.

    2. Andrews’ Six Keys of Occlusion¶

    Clinical Usefulness:¶

    ✅ Finishing Standard – Ensures optimal occlusion after braces/aligners.
    ✅ Precision in Bracket Positioning – Helps in torque, angulation, and archwire adjustments.
    ✅ Functional Occlusion – Prevents post-treatment relapse by ensuring proper cuspal interdigitation.
    ✅ Aesthetic Smile Design – Proper tooth angulation and contacts improve smile harmony.

    Limitations:¶

    ❌ Static Model – Focuses on ideal occlusion but doesn’t account for dynamic jaw movements.
    ❌ Not a Diagnostic Tool – Doesn’t classify malocclusion; only defines what ideal occlusion should be.

    Best for: Final detailing of orthodontic treatment, bracket prescription selection, and finishing cases.

    3. Ackerman-Proffit Classification¶

    Clinical Usefulness:¶

    ✅ Comprehensive Diagnosis – Evaluates malocclusion in all 3 planes (AP, vertical, transverse).
    ✅ Skeletal vs. Dental Analysis – Helps decide if treatment requires:
    - Tooth movement (camouflage orthodontics)
    - Growth modification (functional appliances)
    - Surgery (orthognathic cases)
    ✅ Facial Profile Consideration – Links occlusion to facial aesthetics (e.g., convex profile in Class II).
    ✅ Problem-Oriented Treatment Planning – Identifies primary vs. secondary issues (e.g., crowding + crossbite).

    Limitations:¶

    ❌ More Complex – Requires cephalometric analysis & facial examination (not just models).
    ❌ Less Commonly Used Than Angle’s – More popular in advanced orthodontic practices.

    Best for: Complex cases, surgical planning, and interdisciplinary treatments (e.g., ortho + airway management).

    Comparison of Clinical Applications¶

    Classification Best Used For Limitations
    Angle’s Quick AP diagnosis, simple cases Misses vertical/transverse issues
    Andrews’ Six Keys Finishing occlusion, bracket setup Not for diagnosis
    Ackerman-Proffit Complex cases, surgical planning Requires extra diagnostics

    Conclusion: How They Work Together¶

    1. Angle’s → Gives a starting point (Class I, II, III).
    2. Ackerman-Proffit → Expands to 3D diagnosis (skeletal/dental, facial profile).
    3. Andrews’ Six Keys → Ensures final occlusion is ideal.

    Example:
    - A Class II Division 1 case (Angle’s) may have:
    - Skeletal retrognathia (Ackerman-Proffit) → Needs functional appliance or surgery.
    - Deep bite & crowding → Treated with fixed braces + bite correction.
    - Final occlusion checked with Andrews’ Six Keys for proper torque and contacts.
