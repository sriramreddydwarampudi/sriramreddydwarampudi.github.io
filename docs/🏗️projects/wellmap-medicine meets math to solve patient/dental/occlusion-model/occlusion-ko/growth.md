To truly understand growth, one must first appreciate the ways in which it can occur. Growth isn’t just a simple process of getting bigger—it’s a carefully coordinated biological symphony. Cells can multiply in number, a process known as hyperplasia, or they can swell in size, which we call hypertrophy. Sometimes, growth happens not because of the cells themselves, but because they secrete substances into the space around them—this secretion fills the extracellular matrix and contributes to the overall increase in size, especially in structures like bones and cartilage.

As a child develops, growth doesn’t occur evenly across the body. There are regions where growth is passive, following the lead of other areas, and regions where it is actively controlled. These are known as growth sites and growth centers. Growth centers are like the command posts—they are genetically programmed to grow and influence the rest of the body. Growth sites, on the other hand, respond to external signals or functional demands, but don’t initiate the process themselves.

And growth isn’t stationary—it moves. Imagine bones as sculpted by both building and erosion. When bone is added on one side and taken away on another, this creates a shift called drift. Meanwhile, displacement occurs when the whole structure moves because of growth in an adjacent or connected region—like how the face is pushed forward as the cranial base grows.

As the years go by, growth doesn’t follow a constant rhythm. Instead, it arrives in waves. These are the growth spurts—short periods where the body seems to accelerate its development. Infancy, early childhood, and puberty are the major stages where these bursts happen. Of all, the pubertal spurt is the most significant, especially in clinical practice, because it influences dental and skeletal treatment decisions.

Behind all this is the concept of pattern. Growth pattern isn’t just about how much something grows, but how body parts relate to each other over time. For example, the head is proportionally much larger in infants compared to adults. This change in proportion is part of a larger truth—growth respects the principle of time, the so-called fourth dimension. This pattern, when viewed over years, gives insight into what’s normal and what’s not.

A striking illustration of this is the cephalocaudal gradient of growth—a rule stating that growth happens first in the head and gradually proceeds downward toward the feet. That’s why infants have such large heads and relatively short legs, while adults show a more balanced figure.

Digging deeper into body systems, growth becomes even more specialized. This is known as differential growth. Not all tissues grow at the same pace. In fact, Scammon’s curve of growth vividly shows that each system in the body—neural, lymphoid, general (somatic), and genital—follows its own timeline. Neural tissue grows rapidly in the early years and matures early. Lymphoid tissue grows aggressively in childhood and then shrinks. Somatic growth is steady with a pubertal jump, while genital tissue remains dormant until puberty and then surges.

Even within an individual system, growth doesn’t progress like the ticking of a metronome. Hooton, a notable anthropologist, once remarked that human growth is not a steady, uniform affair. There’s a rhythm to it—surges and pauses, much like the heartbeat. This is best observed in height progression, where some years show great gains and others, only slight.

In all this complexity, how can one tell if a child is growing as expected? This is where growth charts come in. These charts, based on population data, let clinicians compare a child’s growth to what is typical for their age and sex. It’s a vital tool in catching deviations early—whether it’s delayed growth or early onset of puberty.

And perhaps the most profound takeaway is this: to understand abnormalities, one must first understand normality. Only by knowing what growth should look like, can one begin to recognize when it’s going off course.

According to Hooton, "Human growth is not a steady and uniform process, wherein all parts of the body enlarge at the same rate and the increments of I year equal to that of the preceding or succeeding year." However, there seems to be a rhythm during the growth process. This growth rhythm is most clearly seen in stature or body height.
