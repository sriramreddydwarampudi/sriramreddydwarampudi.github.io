Great question! Let's dive into the concepts of Jaati, Chaturasra Jathi, and Janta Swaras in the context of Indian classical music.


---

1. Jaati (जाति) in Indian Music:

Jaati refers to the number of beats in a unit of rhythm. It determines the length of a rhythm cycle and is closely related to the time signature in Western music. Jaati is used to define the structure of a tāla (rhythmic cycle).

There are different Jaatis based on the number of beats:

Tisra Jaati (3 beats)

Chaturasra Jaati (4 beats)

Khanda Jaati (5 beats)

Mishra Jaati (7 beats)

Sankirna Jaati (9 beats)


Each Jaati can be applied to different tālas (rhythmic cycles) such as Teentaal (16 beats), Adachaap (8 beats), etc.


---

2. Chaturasra Jaati (चतुरस्र जाति):

Chaturasra Jaati refers to 4-beat rhythm and is used in Chaturasra Tāla (4-beat time cycle). This is the most commonly used Jaati in classical music.

Chaturasra means "fourfold" (from Sanskrit "chatur" meaning four).

The beats of the cycle are divided into 4 equal parts, which gives a simple and balanced rhythm.


Example of Chaturasra Jaati:

Tāla: Adi Tāla (8 beats), divided into 4 beats per matra (beat). This allows each beat to be evenly spaced out in a 4-part cycle.


Rhythmic Phrase Example (Chaturasra Jaati):

1st Beat: Ta

2nd Beat: Ka

3rd Beat: Di

4th Beat: Mi


This gives the Chaturasra rhythm, which is often used in popular forms of Carnatic and Hindustani classical music.


---

3. Janta Swaras (जन्त स्वार):

Janta Swaras are longer (doubled) notes used in the varnams and other forms of classical compositions. The term "Janta" means "long" in Sanskrit.

The Janta Swaras are sung or played for twice the usual duration of regular notes.


Example of Janta Swaras in a Raga (for practice):

Sa – Sa

Ri – Ri

Ga – Ga
(Here, each swara is held for double the time compared to normal swaras)


This technique is used to build more emphasis on each note, making the phrase slow and emphatic, often for the beginning sections of a raga or during specific ornamentations.


---

Relation Between Jaati, Janta Swaras, and Rāga:

Jaati defines how many beats fit into a cycle (important for setting the rhythm of a composition).

Chaturasra Jaati typically uses 4 beats per cycle (in both simple and complex rhythmic compositions).

Janta Swaras are a technique used to lengthen the notes, giving more emphasis to each swara.

A Varnam (वरनम्) is a crucial and distinctive form of composition in Carnatic music (South Indian classical music). It serves as an introduction and exploration of the raga (melody) and is often performed in the middle of a concert. A Varnam typically includes a blend of lyrical beauty and rhythmic complexity, and it is a vital element in the training of a classical musician.

Key Features of a Varnam:

1. Structure:

A Varnam is divided into two parts: the Pallavi (refrain) and Anupallavi (verse). These are the core parts of the composition.

Charanam (optional) is also included, providing further elaboration on the raga.

In Carnatic music, Varnams can be set to a variety of tālas (rhythmic cycles), typically ranging from Adi Tāla (8 beats) to Chaturasra Jaati (4 beats) and others.



2. Text and Meaning:

The lyrics (or sahityam) of a Varnam are usually in Sanskrit, Telugu, or Tamil.

The text often consists of repetitive phrases that provide a chance for a musician to demonstrate intricate rhythmic and melodic improvisation.



3. Lyrical and Melodic Components:

The Varnam's Pallavi usually introduces the raga and its key svaras (notes).

The Anupallavi and Charanam sections develop and expand the raga, allowing for graceful improvisation while staying true to the melodic structure.

It incorporates Janta Swaras, svaras (notes), and melismatic phrases (multiple notes sung in quick succession).



4. Role in Performance:

Varnams are often the first item in a concert, setting the stage for the rest of the performance.

In the madhyama kala (medium tempo), they are energetic and can be fast-paced, but they can also be performed in slow tempos for emotional depth.



5. Varnam in Rhythm:

Many Varnams are composed in tālas like Adi Tāla (8 beats), Roopaka Tāla (6 beats), and Khanda Tāla (5 beats).

The rhythmic cycle of a Varnam is usually complicated, incorporating a range of rhythmic patterns and improvisation.




Types of Varnams:

Pada Varnam: This includes more emphasis on lyrics and is often used for dancing. It is more melodic than rhythmic.

Alankara Varnam: A focus on the raga and tāla (rhythm), allowing the musician to explore the aesthetic nature of the raga and cycle.

Sahitya Varnam: This incorporates lyrics to provide a spiritual meaning, often connected to devotional content.



---

Example of a Varnam:

Let’s take a popular Varnam in Carnatic music: "Vandanam" in Raga Hamsadhwani and Adi Tāla.

Varnam Structure:

Pallavi: This will introduce the raga Hamsadhwani and the thematic content (devotional praise).

Anupallavi: Develops further the raga, with extended melodic phrases.

Charanam: Builds upon the previous parts, often with deeper improvisations.


Sahityam (Lyric Example):

"Vandanam Gopalana - Vandanam"
(Praise to Lord Gopala)

In this case, the Pallavi and Anupallavi are repeated multiple times, and the raga is explored by elaborating on these sections in different ways through improvisation.


---

Varnam as a Training Tool:

Varnams are an excellent practice tool for both vocalists and instrumentalists in Carnatic music. They help musicians to:

Develop their technical skills (by practicing rhythmic patterns).

Explore the nuances of the raga through improvisation.

Train their sense of timing and tāla (rhythmic cycle).

Improve their expression through lyrical phrasing.
